01 · Broca's area

Speech

Most of my research asks what someone's speech says about their brain: who has aphasia and how severe it is, how autistic kids talk with clinicians, and which variant of a dementia a patient has.

Notes and scans, together

UCSF NeuroAI Lab · RISE intern · 2025

Primary progressive aphasia comes in three variants that are hard to tell apart. I trained a CLIP-style model that pulls a patient's clinical notes and MRI into the same space. It separated the variants better than the scan alone. I was the only high schooler among the 20 RISE interns, most of whom were medical students. I also built speech-quality agents for the Memory and Aging Center's diagnostic copilot. code

Reading aphasia from a transcript

Boston University · Center for Brain Recovery · 2024–25

A language model that reads a transcript of someone with aphasia, names the type, and estimates severity on the Western Aphasia Battery. It was tested on 61 patients, and a first-author paper is in progress. I also wrote the lab's Python neuroimaging pipeline. code

Speech in autism

Stanford Speech and Social Neuroscience Lab · 2025–

I adapted a speech-analysis tool I wrote in high school for the lab's Autism Speaker Study, and I'm working toward a speech-based diagnostic measure that holds up across populations.

Aphasia across languages

independent · 2023–25

How do aphasia symptoms change from one language to another? I used language models to compare them, and trained a model that predicts resting-state brain connectivity from demographics, to simulate how recovery might go. code

Presented at the Academy of Aphasia, the Cognitive Neuroscience Society, and the American Academy of Neurology, among others. I won the AAN's Neuroscience Research Prize in 2024, one of three nationally.

03 · hippocampus

Memory

I write short memoir, mostly about my family and the odd ways we get through things. Two pieces won Scholastic Gold Keys, and one placed in the top 15% of the New York Times 100-Word Memoir contest.

a hundred-word memoir · on a wildfire

The sky bruised red, the kind of red that stings. And I just sat. My screen flickered with muted classmates. My parents ripped apart the house, stuffing jeans, cramming socks, tossing our mundane lives into suitcases. What about my Legos? Our grandfather clock, unticking? Would it all burn? I didn't move. Didn't breathe. The fire licked its lips, devouring faded golds and muted browns of the Diablo Range.

Then, the inferno retreated, granting a reprieve. The smoke dissipated, threads dissolving into pale sky. Pandemic rhythms resumed their monotone pulse.

My seven-year-old sister split the quiet: "I wanted to go to a hotel!"

For the Birds

After a fight with my mom, a mockingbird family nests in our pomelo tree, and I start getting up at 3 a.m. to guard it.

I set my alarm for 3 a.m., waking to stand guard every morning.

Sheared Solidarity

My grandfather vows to shave his head if my dad recovers, and I try to square that with what I believe about science.

Maybe that's what life was: a messy intersection of science and belief, data and prayer, fear and hope.
